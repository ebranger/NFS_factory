This is a list of things that could be improved.

A Linux build (CMake). The code has fallbacks for the Windows-specific parts (free host memory, file positions), but the
binary relation files are read with fread there, without the read-ahead of the Windows build, and it has not been built or tested.

The 3LP candidate file (3lp: save) holds a in 32 bits, as the .bin format, so the candidates of relations with |a| of 2^31 or
more (from .ab or text files) are skipped and counted. With 3lp: auto they are checked. A candidate format with a 64-bit a
would keep them.

The other side's factor lists for .ab files are what a run on an RTX 5090 spends most on after the trees: for x - 10^53 with a
polynomial of degree 4 (24 ns per relation checked in all, 7.2 relations found per 1000), 5.1 ns, against 19 ns without them. Of
that, 1.2 ns are the batch check of the norms (other_stage: the tree against the primes up to 2^25, and rho on the cofactors of
two larger primes), 2.0 ns the factoring of the part of each norm below that bound (k_factor_walk: Pollard rho with a thread per
value, mostly with two limbs), and 1.8 ns the 18 % of the norms with three or more larger primes, whose cofactor and rest are
factored apart in jobs (1.4 us per norm, five times what the others cost). Checking the norms against several bounds in the same
tree (the product tree is shared, each bound costs a remainder tree) would give the part of each norm between two bounds, which
is mostly a prime, or a product of two that rho splits with one limb; an estimate is half of the 5.1 ns. The bound itself
(OTHER_STAGE_PRIMES_ABOVE) is a compromise: for x - 10^53 2^25 was 4 % faster than 2^26, for x^7 - 30 2^25 was 4 % faster than
the 2^23 that is chosen.

Pollard rho on the cofactors of two large primes of the checked side (k_rho) costs 2.2 ns of the 16 ns per relation of a run
with "format: keys" (x - 10^53, lpba 29: about 8400 cofactors per million relations, of which a third are relations). The
cofactors that are not relations could be found by a batch check of their own against the primes up to 2^lpb (as the 3LP second
stage does), leaving rho only for the relations, whose two primes are printed; their batches would have to wait for it, which
changes the order of the work, not of the output.

A validating converter to .ab (finding the right a for the relations of old .bin files whose |a| is 2^31 or more, which needs the
polynomial) exists outside the repository; Utilities\RelationsToBinaryFormat converts without checking.

The 3LP second stage runs in the thread that checks the batches of the first stage, which waits for it, and the candidates in
an unfinished second stage batch are not part of the resume position: if a run is interrupted, they are lost.

Batch checking with early abort: a first batch against the primes up to about 2^20 only (a much smaller tree), and the full
check only for the few values whose remaining part can still be smooth. Perhaps 15-25 % faster, but it loses some relations,
and the best split depends on parameters that change during a factorization.

GPU transform kernels: they are limited by latency (registers, barriers between the passes), not memory bandwidth. Passes of 4
levels with 16 elements per thread would need fewer barriers, at the cost of registers. A few percent at most. The transforms
(kRow, kCol) are about two thirds of the time of the kernels of a tree, the combine and its carries a sixth; the products of
small numbers, the leaves and the root the rest. The kernels of the trees of several threads take turns on the GPU rather than
run at once, so the time of a run is close to the sum of the times of its kernels: a tree of 2^18 leaves has about 400 kernel
launches, many of them of a few us, which larger trees spread over more relations (see treelevels).

The plan of the integer NTT (number of primes and transform length) is chosen with a cost model of the CPU transforms. On the
GPU the CRT combine is a larger part, and grows with the square of the number of primes.

The prime product of the 3LP second stage (all primes below 2^lbp) takes about 1.3 s to set up on the CPU (the root tests of
28 million primes). Only noticeable on short runs.

On the CPU still: the check of the saved factor lists of the relations found (.bin and text files), relation values of more than
256 bits, other side norms of more than 256 bits, and tinyqs. For .ab files the polynomial values are computed on the GPU; for
.bin and text files they still are computed on the CPU, a relation at a time, and the reader adds those relations one at a time
(about 50 ns each, which limits such a run to about 20 million relations per second).

With -rels, a run stops after the batches in progress, so it finds more relations than asked for: up to twelve batches with
small trees, or four to six of a million relations each with the large ones (about 30000 relations for x - 10^53).

The 3LP second stage needs the product of all primes below 2^lpb, so three large primes are limited to lpb 32 (and its candidate
file to primes of 32 bits: a candidate whose saved list of the other side has a larger prime is kept without the list, which is
rebuilt from the norm). The .bin format holds primes of 32 bits too: a relation set sieved with a larger bound on the side
that is not checked needs the .ab format.

A cofactor of more than 2 log2(lim) bits is taken as two large primes, also when it is below 2^lpb. With lim below 2^(lpb/2)
(131072 for lpb 34, far below any lim in use) a relation with one large prime above lim^2 is therefore not found. The limit
could be max(2 log2(lim), lpb) bits.
